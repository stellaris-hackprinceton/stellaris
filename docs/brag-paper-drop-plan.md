## Creative premise

Stellaris takes a small physical question through sources, a testable hypothesis, executable simulation, real evidence, and a scientific interpretation in one project.

Research question: **How does changing the shape of an equal sheet of paper affect its fall time from a fixed height? Can a simple drag model explain the differences?**

Compare flat, accordion-folded, and crumpled sheets. The expected direction is that a compact shape often falls faster than a broad flat sheet. The ordering of all three shapes, the size of the differences, and the adequacy of the model are experimental questions. Do not script the answer.

Hook: close shot of identical sheets becoming three shapes. Text: **“Same paper. Different fall?”**

Ending: the real measured comparison beside the recording and interpretation. Text: **“Ask. Test. Understand.”** Then Stellaris and “From question to discovery.”

## Scientific protocol to prepare before filming

Supplies: identical sheets from one paper pack, phone or webcam, stable camera support, ruler or tape measure, and an uncluttered indoor drop area. No added weights or specialized sensors.

Use three equal sheets, one per shape. Keep all paper in each shape and add no tape or clips. Equal stock and dimensions provide nominally equal mass without requiring a scale. Record this assumption rather than claiming measured equality.

Choose a reproducible release reference and a measured drop height, roughly 1–1.5 m if the camera can see the whole fall. Document how each shape is aligned with the reference and note the finite size of the flat sheet. Release without an intentional push, with the flat sheet initially horizontal and the folded shape in a documented orientation.

Film three drops per shape, nine trials total, in an interleaved order. Use a fixed camera, preferably 60 fps or higher, with release and floor contact visible. Save originals, actual media timestamps, trial labels, shape photographs, dimensions, height, and deviations. Account for variable frame timing where present. Identify obstructed or ambiguous trials explicitly and preserve them.

Primary measurement: elapsed time from release to first floor contact, with release and contact frames saved as evidence. Manual frame annotation is acceptable if clearly shown and supported by the actual workflow. It must not be presented as automatic tracking. Prefer reliable timing to a fragile full-trajectory detector.

Analysis: show individual trial times and the mean for each shape, with trial spread. Estimate event-timing uncertainty from frame timestamps and ambiguity in the event selection. Do not treat trial spread as equivalent to timing uncertainty. With three trials per shape, describe this as a small exploratory experiment.

Simulation: execute a simple vertical fall model with gravity and quadratic drag. Sweep a range of effective drag-to-mass values, including a no-drag baseline. Label outputs “Simulation” and disclose the assumed parameter range. Before measurements, these are possible behaviors, not calibrated numerical predictions for the three shapes. The range and model must be saved before inspecting results if presented as a prior prediction.

The physical experiment supplies information the simulation lacks: actual fall times, orientation changes, flutter, and trial variability. A constant-drag model may capture a trend without reproducing a flat sheet's motion. Do not infer a uniquely measured drag coefficient or terminal speed from these short drops. Any later fit must be labeled a fit, not an independent prediction.
